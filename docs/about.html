Where PlanForge comes from

PlanForge began as a Rust port of Numeric Fast Downward, which is itself a fork of Fast Downward. The line of descent runs in that order, and naming only the last link would misdescribe it:

PlanForge ← Numeric Fast Downward ← Fast Downward

The middle link is the one that matters most here. Mainline Fast Downward has no numeric support at all: numeric fluents, numeric conditions, comparison axioms and the numeric heuristics that this planner is mostly made of come from Numeric Fast Downward, not from mainline. So Numeric Fast Downward is the direct predecessor, and mainline is its ancestor rather than ours.

What the port kept is more than an idea. The task representation, the SAS+ translation pipeline, the shape of the statistics block the planner prints, and several of the numeric heuristics follow the predecessor closely, and the code says so where it matters: numeric LM-cut, the numeric bounds, the successor generator and the abstraction machinery carry explicit PARITY(numeric-fd) comments marking the places where the Rust behaviour is deliberately identical to the reference implementation, quirks included, so that a difference in a plan cost can be attributed rather than guessed at. Where the two implementations do disagree, the disagreement is treated as a bug in one of them and tracked, not quietly normalised away.

Numeric Fast Downward

Numeric Fast Downward is published at github.com/dgnad/numeric-fast-downward, and an earlier public tree of the same project is at github.com/Kurorororo/numeric-fast-downward; both are under the GNU GPL v3. The particular working tree PlanForge was ported from is a private repository, so it is not linked here — at the file the port follows most closely, it is identical to the second of those two.

Its history begins with an initial commit by Chiara Piacentini in September 2020 and continues to the end of 2024; by commit count the bulk of the work in that tree is by Ryo Kuroiwa and Daniel Gnad, with further contributions from Eyal Weiss and Misha Gruntov. That history is squashed, so it records who worked on the tree but not the full authorship of what came before it; the credit above should be read as "these are the people the repository names", not as a complete author list for numeric Fast Downward as a body of work.

Its numeric heuristics implement published results. These are the references carried in Numeric Fast Downward's own plugin documentation — that is, the papers the predecessor's code cites for itself, transcribed rather than inferred:

  • C. Piacentini, M. P. Castro, A. A. Ciré and J. C. Beck, “Linear and Integer Programming-Based Heuristics for Cost-Optimal Numeric Planning”, AAAI, 2018, pp. 6254–6261.
  • C. Piacentini, M. P. Castro, A. A. Ciré and J. C. Beck, “Compiling Optimal Numeric Planning to Mixed Integer Linear Programming”, ICAPS, 2018, pp. 383–387.
  • E. Scala, P. Haslum, D. Magazzeni and S. Thiébaux, “Landmarks for Numeric Planning Problems”, IJCAI, 2017.
  • E. Scala, P. Haslum, S. Thiébaux and M. Ramirez, “Subgoaling Techniques for Satisficing and Optimal Numeric Planning”, Journal of Artificial Intelligence Research 68, 2020, pp. 691–752.

Not every one of these corresponds to something PlanForge implements — the mixed-integer-programming compilations in particular have no counterpart here. They are listed because they are the numeric-planning results the predecessor is built on, and the port inherits its design from that work. Numeric Fast Downward's own source does not name a publication for its numeric LM-cut, which is the heuristic PlanForge follows most closely, so no citation is offered for it here rather than a guessed one.

What came from current mainline Fast Downward

The translator is not a port of the fork's 2015-era translator. Where mainline Fast Downward has since changed how translation works, PlanForge follows the current design, and the source cites the mainline issues it is matching: issue453 and issue454 for axiom handling — every axiom proves its head, and default-value axioms move into the SAS task — and issue879 for keeping invariant-candidate order deterministic so that the SAS variables do not depend on the run. The strongly-connected-component analysis is Tarjan's algorithm written iteratively, for the same reason mainline made that change: recursion depth on large tasks. This is why the translator resembles current Fast Downward more than it resembles the fork it descends from.

What the port changed is the whole of the implementation. There is no C++ and no Python in the pipeline: the PDDL reader, the grounder, the invariant analysis, the search engines, the abstractions and the state registry are Rust, and several capabilities on this site — the Cartesian abstractions, the saturated cost partitionings, the gradient engine — have no counterpart in either predecessor. Being a derivative work all the same, PlanForge carries its predecessors' copyleft licensing forward, as described below.

Prior work the implementation follows

Several components implement published algorithms. These are other people's results, and PlanForge claims none of them; the citations below are the ones recorded in the source files that implement them, so that a reader can go from a module to the paper it came from. No abstracts, figures or text from any of these works are reproduced here or in the repository.

  • The FF heuristic. J. Hoffmann and B. Nebel, “The FF Planning System”, Journal of Artificial Intelligence Research, 2001. PlanForge's ff heuristic is this relaxed-plan heuristic.
  • Numeric relaxation for FF. J. Hoffmann, “The Metric-FF Planning System”, Journal of Artificial Intelligence Research, 2003. The monotonic numeric relaxation that gives each numeric variable a reachable envelope through the relaxed planning graph follows this work.
  • Post-hoc optimisation. F. Pommerening, G. Röger and M. Helmert, “Getting the Most Out of Pattern Databases for Classical Planning”, IJCAI, 2013. The posthoc_optimization heuristic solves this LP over a CEGAR-built abstraction collection.
  • Split selection for Cartesian abstractions. Schindler, Speck and Helmert, ICAPS, 2026. The icaps26_cartesian abstraction source implements the full-task, first-flaw and desired-region split policies from this paper. (Title and full author names to be added: the repository records the authors and the venue but not the title, and it is not guessed here.)

Benchmarks

No benchmark set is redistributed with PlanForge or from this site. The small PDDL tasks in the repository are regression fixtures with pinned optimal costs, not a benchmark suite. For the numeric domains themselves, see the International Planning Competition pages and the collected PDDL instance repositories.

Licence

The planner

PlanForge is free software under version 3 of the GNU copyleft licences, split by crate. The license field of each crate manifest is authoritative; at the time of writing it is:

LicenceCrates
LGPL-3.0 planforge-sas, planforge-translate, planforge-search, planforge-config-derive, planforge-cplex — the libraries.
GPL-3.0 planforge, planforge-searcher, planforge-sgd, planforge-py, tests — the binaries, the Python bindings and the integration tests.

In practice: the planner you run is covered by the GPL v3, and the library crates you might link into your own project are covered by the LGPL v3, which is the GPL v3 plus additional permissions for linking. The repository's LICENSE file carries the GNU General Public License v3 text; the Lesser GPL is defined on top of it and both are linked above. If you are deciding what you may do, read the crate manifest for the crate you actually depend on.

Why it is copyleft

The obligation is inherited, not chosen. Fast Downward is distributed under the GNU General Public License v3, Numeric Fast Downward carries the same licence — its public repositories ship the GPL v3 text and are marked GPL-3.0 — and PlanForge is a port of that fork. The copyleft flows down the whole chain, which is why the licence table above is GPL and LGPL rather than something more permissive.

One detail, for completeness: the private working tree PlanForge was actually ported from carries no top-level licence file of its own. The statement above therefore rests on the licence of the public Numeric Fast Downward repositories, which is where that code is published, rather than on a declaration inside the tree itself.

Contact

There is no mailing list. Bug reports, questions about a configuration, and patches all belong in the issue tracker on the GitHub repository. A report that includes the domain and problem files, the exact --search string and the planner's output is one somebody can act on.
